AsksBack grades the written exam, and where the paper is not enough evidence, it asks the student a short written follow-up with graded hints. The teacher gets what a grade hides: how close each student is, and how much help they need.

Right, but why?

“It is O(log n).” Full marks for the result, and no evidence the student knows why. The answer alone hides it (Treagust, 1988).

Same score, different students

One 7/11 needs a nudge; another needs the concept again. On paper they look the same.

Who wrote this?

Some answers read unlike the rest of the student's exam. A grade cannot tell you whether they understand it.

An oral exam would settle it, but teachers don't have the time. AsksBack asks only where the paper failed as evidence, in writing, in a few minutes.

Scoring is code

The model only judges whether a reply meets a rubric criterion. Weights, scores and profiles are plain arithmetic the teacher can read and change.

Works without AI

Mock mode reads the handwriting locally and uses teacher-written hints. A real model writes the questions and judges replies.

The teacher decides

Every profile comes with its reason and the full transcript. The teacher's judgment is recorded next to it.

Step 1 · Scan and grade

Flip through the pile with a phone.

  • Each photo is matched to its page and straightened, even upside down or out of order.
  • The name box is read and matched to the class roster.
  • Each open answer is graded against the rubric, with a confidence and whether the reasoning is on paper.

The demo: an algorithms quiz, four multiple-choice questions and three open ones, with five handwritten papers and fifteen simulated classmates.

G5

Uncertain authorship

Much more elaborate than the student's other answers.

A new case to apply it to

The question type is fixed by the trigger; only the wording adapts. G5 never accuses: the student just gets a fresh case, and the profile says “verify understanding”.

Step 3 · Ask back

Bruno's follow-up, as he saw it.

He wrote only the result. The first question quotes him and asks why. His first reply is vague, so he gets an implicit hint, then reaches it. One more question, with no help, checks that it transfers.

  • Every question is checked before it is sent: kind, needed, quotes the student, open, one idea, no giveaway.
  • A question that fails is rewritten; after two tries, the teacher's own hint is used.
  • 15 minutes in total, starting when the student presses Start.
The mediation ladder

The less help needed, the more credit.

Nearly the answer.

T

Transfer

A new case, unaided. Failing it drops the credit one level.

Real

The written score (adjusted if a G3 clarification shows the answer was right).

Mediated

Real plus the recovered points, weighted by the level at which they were reached.

Potential

(mediated − real) / (max − real): the share of the missing points recovered.

The weights are design choices to calibrate, not values from the literature. Teachers can change them, and the class is rescored.

Why this measures better

Built on four lines of research.

Two-tier diagnostics

Asking for the reason exposes misconceptions the answer hides. Treagust (1988); Tsui & Treagust (2010).

Dynamic assessment

How much help a student needs shows their zone of proximal development. Actual, mediated and learning-potential scores: Poehner & Lantolf (2013).

Interactive orals

A personal conversation is hard to outsource, and students perform better in it. Sotiriadou et al. (2019); Huxham et al. (2012).

AI follow-ups work

Socratic Mind (Georgia Tech, 2025) and work on follow-up quality: benevolent, necessary, contextual, open (Scientific Reports, 2026).

None of them couples dynamic assessment to a written exam that is already graded. That is the gap.
